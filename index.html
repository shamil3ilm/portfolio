      Delegated access · Featured

      Controlled access to other people's money

      Payroll and bookkeeping companies needed to work inside their customers' accounts, where one mistake moves real money. I built the layer that lets them, without ever holding a customer's login: every action is recorded, and every refusal looks the same from outside.

      ProblemPayroll and bookkeeping providers needed to work inside their customers' accounts through the existing payment APIs, without sharing logins and without being able to sign off the customer's own payments.

      ApproachAn authorization layer added to the existing APIs, so providers and customers go through the same rules. Access is granted by the customer, every call is attributed and audited, and payments that need the customer's own approvers stay theirs.

      Read the case study →Try the demo →
      • KeyAccess starts with a single-use, expiring key the customer creates
      • LogEvery call is attributed to the provider and recorded, allowed or refused
      • DenyEvery refusal looks the same from outside; the real reason goes to the audit log
      Payment approvals

      Approved means unchanged

      A payment could be approved for one bank account and sent to another if the details changed in between. Now the destination is locked at approval and checked again before money moves.

      ProblemIn a multi-level approval flow, a recipient's bank details could change after a payment was submitted, so approvers could sign off one destination while the money went to another.

      ApproachThe details that decide where money lands are fingerprinted at submission and re-checked at release. Recipient and bank-account edits are blocked while a payment is pending, and every refused approval returns a specific reason instead of a generic failure.

      Try the demo →
      Recurring payments

      Schedules that stop when they should

      Deleting a recipient or bank account didn't stop the payments scheduled to it. Now it stops every one of them, and says so.

      ProblemDeleting a recipient or bank account didn't stop the recurring payments tied to it, and cancelling several schedules at once left their upcoming payments queued.

      ApproachDeleting now warns first, then cancels every linked schedule with an audit note, as one all-or-nothing change. Bulk deletes go through the same per-record path, and bulk cancel clears every selected schedule.

      Scheduling

      Evening fraud-check files that never ran

      BeforeA daily Positive Pay file (the list banks use to catch forged checks; the platform produces them for 17 banks) set for after about 8 PM US Eastern was matched against the next day in UTC, and silently skipped.

      AfterThe run time is now read against today's date in the account's own time zone, so evening runs go out on the right day.

      How I work

      Design first. Prove it. Then ship it.

      I plan the work before I write it, prove it with tests, and review every change. I use AI coding tools to move faster, but I only ship what I understand and can explain.

      1. 1. Design before codeA short written design and a plan, with the checks that will prove each step.
      2. 2. Tests firstI write the failing test before the fix, so I know the fix works and the bug can't quietly return.
      3. 3. Real checks, not guessesBefore anything counts as done I run it for real: the full test suite, a browser, and read-only queries against real data.
      4. 4. Every change reviewedEvery change, however it was written, is read line by line. Nothing ships that I can't explain.

      Example: self-hosted AI model support for lee, my job-search assistant: a design, failing tests, the implementation, then the whole test suite passing before it merged. See the code ↗
